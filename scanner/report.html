Anomalies — what is odd this year

Japan is fiscally tightening, not loosening
On 214% debt at 2.7% inflation Japan's −1.7% nominal deficit is a +3.9% REAL surplus. Eight economies flip sign once the inflation gain is counted: Egypt, Pakistan, Nigeria, Japan, Canada, Netherlands, South Korea. Every conventional sectoral-balance chart shows these as expansionary; on Godley's own accounting they are not.
Three savers are simultaneously hoarding and fragile
China, Hong Kong and South Korea are in the savers-trap and Ponzi-financed — the private sector is accumulating surpluses while its financing still requires rollover plus fresh borrowing to stand. Normally hoarding and fragility are opposites; here they coexist.
The Anglo bloc stopped being fragile and nobody noticed
UK −40pp, Canada −27pp, US −22pp, Australia −20pp of private credit/GDP off their peaks. On measured financing structure they are hedge-financed — the opposite of the received narrative, and consistent with Levy's own October 2025 finding on corporate net liabilities.
Hong Kong and Ireland are 12–16pp away from their own norms
Hong Kong's private balance is +15.7pp above its historical norm and Ireland's +11.5pp — the two largest sector dislocations in the panel, both entrepôts where the number reflects offshore structures rather than domestic behaviour.
Saudi Arabia's private sector swung 8.5pp below its norm
The largest deterioration on the board, and the reason it classifies as forced-borrower: the private sector is absorbing the adjustment while the state expands.
Twelve economies share one configuration
A = 3.58, and the savers-trap carries the largest systemic load (index 1.58 × 12 copies). One country escapes a savers-trap by widening its external surplus — which must come out of another's. The US is already absorbing $1,080bn of the world's surpluses.

3 · Inflation accounting

Godley & Cripps (1983:245): “the faster the rate of inflation the larger the government’s cash deficit must be in order to keep real debt constant.” The adjustment is symmetric across sectors and sums to zero, so the identity survives — it changes the level and sometimes the sign of each balance, never the constraint.

6 · World consistency — the redundant-equation check

Godley left the world identity out of the solved system and used it as a diagnostic (“an accounting system with no black holes”). Benchmarked against the measured global discrepancy, not zero.

7 · What must give

GermanyGermany runs a private balance of +8.5% of GDP (from +5.7% four years ago), a government balance of -2.7% (expanding), and an external balance of -5.8%. The private retrenchment is being absorbed by the government deficit. If fiscal consolidates before private saving normalises, income must fall to close the identity. There is no fourth option -- the identity must hold.
JapanJapan runs a private balance of +6.2% of GDP (from +6.2% four years ago), a government balance of -1.7% (consolidating), and an external balance of -4.5%. On current policy the configuration can persist; trend growth implies a private balance of +8.9% of GDP, within reach of +6.2%. There is no fourth option -- the identity must hold.
United StatesUnited States runs a private balance of +3.9% of GDP (from -0.1% four years ago), a government balance of -7.9% (expanding), and an external balance of +4.0%. The private retrenchment is being absorbed by the government deficit. If fiscal consolidates before private saving normalises, income must fall to close the identity. There is no fourth option -- the identity must hold.
United KingdomUnited Kingdom runs a private balance of +3.1% of GDP (from +2.7% four years ago), a government balance of -6.1% (expanding), and an external balance of +3.0%. Growth currently rests on the fiscal injection. Withdraw it before private spending or net exports take over, and income falls. There is no fourth option -- the identity must hold.
ChinaChina runs a private balance of +9.3% of GDP (from +9.7% four years ago), a government balance of -7.1% (neutral), and an external balance of -2.2%. To grow at trend the private sector would have to absorb +8.8% of GDP. Since a surplus that large cannot be spent into existence, either the government must accommodate, or the external surplus must widen, or income must fall. There is no fourth option -- the identity must hold.
South KoreaSouth Korea runs a private balance of +6.1% of GDP (from +2.8% four years ago), a government balance of -0.8% (consolidating), and an external balance of -5.3%. To grow at trend the private sector would have to absorb +5.7% of GDP. Since a surplus that large cannot be spent into existence, either the government must accommodate, or the external surplus must widen, or income must fall. There is no fourth option -- the identity must hold.
Balances from IMF fiscal and current-account actuals and Eurostat annual sector accounts; credit from BIS; norms are each sector’s own pre-2020 median. The three balances sum to zero by construction — the identity is a frame, not evidence. Conditional, not predictive: the report states what must happen if a configuration persists, not when.
